Critical analysis of literature on climate change communication 

I. The Evolution of Communication on Climate Change

1. The Framing Paradigm
At first glance, public communication was based on the assumption that the public lacked information and that providing more of it would heighten their concern about climate change. Research on framing paradigms starts from the opposite observation: the same facts can have different meanings depending on how they are presented. Thus, Nisbet concludes that the way climate change is presented influences engagement just as much as the content of the message itself.

2. Misinformation
Misinformation and fake news are very common when it comes to climate change. This greatly impacts people’s views on climate change.
according to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), "misinformation on climate change and the deliberate undermining of science have contributed to misperceptions of the scientific consensus, uncertainty, disregarded risk and urgency, and dissent”.
Misleading content distorts perceptions of climate science and proposed solutions, and sows confusion.

3. From Fear to Effectiveness
Alarmist portrayals grab attention but prove ineffective at motivating personal engagement. According to O’Neill & Nicholson-Cole, non-threatening images linked to people’s everyday concerns elicit greater engagement: “Fear won’t do it.” This is the main argument in favor of the “positive message” that underpins our project: pages that emphasize what can be done should mobilize people more than those that highlight only the threat.

4. From Denial to Procrastination
Giving people more onformation does not automatically lead to action. Kari Norgaard, after studying a Norwegian community during a surprising warml winter, believes that the public’s inaction is not due to ignorance, but to an information overload, which produces the exact opposite of the intended effect. People tend to reject climate change once they realize there is no simple, miracle solution. As they come to understand the complexity of the problem, they feel overwhelmed and leads to social denial.
Lamb et al. argue that opposition has shifted from denying climate change to accepting it, while simultaneously slowing down action by emphasizing the social costs of climate policy and questioning the feasibility of mitigation measures. A message can therefore appear constructive while actually serving to delay action.

5. The Geographical and Political Context
Hase et al. show that media coverage is much more extensive in the Northern Countries than in the Southern Countries. Furthermore, it appears that the attention given to these topics varies by country and follows the political agenda; Northern countries address climate change more frequently, while Southern countries place greater emphasis on societal challenges and their implications.


II. Critical Analysis and Discussion

Most of the research draws on Western news articles in English and survey data. However, as we have seen, perspectives can vary greatly from one country to another.

The overall trend in climate change communication has thus shifted from information to action, moving from a message centered on fear to one that combines a sense of threat with a focus on effectiveness.
